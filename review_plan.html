The special importance of large and sparse matrices

large and sparse matrices arise naturally in many settings, e.g. determining the stationary temperature distribution in a two-dimenstional plate.

Iterative methods and subspaces exploit sparsity. This is more effective than simply extending Gaussian elimination to bigger matrices.

Relationship Between Chapters

orthogonalization and projection are the unifying ideas behind much of the course.

Orthogonalization provides suitable bases for subspaces. Projection reduces large computational problems to smaller ones.

A Krylov subspace has the form:

\[ \mathcal K_m(A,v) =\operatorname{span}\{v,Av,A^2v,\ldots,A^{m-1}v\}. \]

This same construction appears in both large sparse linear-system solvers an dlarge sparse eigensovles. The same Arnoldi or Lanczos machinery can support both kinds of computation.

Especially three concepts deserve extra attention:

Orthogonality, projection and subspaces

Suppose \(Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\) has orthonormal columns:

\[ Q^TQ=I_m. \]

Then the matrix

\[ P=QQ^T \]

is the orthogonal projector onto the subspace spanned by the columns of \(Q\).

Eigenvalues, diagonalization and the spectral theorem

For a general diagonalizable matrix,

\[ A=V\Lambda V^{-1}. \]

For a real symmetric matrix, the much stronger result

\[ A=Q\Lambda Q^T,\qquad Q^TQ=I. \]

Is the spectral theorem. It t ells us that a symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and admits an orthonormal basis or eigenvectors.

Consider an inital vector

\[ v=\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_iq_i, \]

where \(q_i\) are the orthonormal eigenvectors of \(A\). Then

\[ A^kv=\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i\lambda_i^kq_i. \]

Applying the matrix repeatedly changes the relative importance of the eigenvector accordidng to the magnitued of their eigenvalues. This explains the fundamental mechanism of power iteration and, after generalization, subspace iteration and polynomial filtering.

Symmetric positive definiteness and quadratic forms

A real symmetric matrix \(A\) is positive definite when

\[ x^TAx>0\qquad\text{for every }x\ne0. \]

Equivalent characterizations include the positivity of all eigenvalues and the existence of a Cholesky factorization.

The relationship between SPD matrices and minimization problems is especially useful. For example, solving \(Ax = b\) for an SPD matrix is equivalnet to minimizing

\[ f(x)=\frac12x^TAx-b^Tx. \]

This provides a natural connection between linear systems, quadratic optimization and the conjugate gradient method.

Conditioning versus stability

Conditioning is a property of a mathematical problem. Stability is a property of an algorithm used to solve that problem.

For instance, suppose \(Ax = b\) and we perturb the right-hand side by \(\Delta b\). The solution changes according to

\[ \Delta x = A^{-1} \Delta b \]

The condition number

\[ \kappa(A)=\|A\|\|A^{-1}\| \]

measures the potential amplification of relative errors. But even if a problem is well-conditioned, a poor algorithm can introduce unnecessarily large computational errors.

It is alos essential to distinguish the residual

\[ r=b-A\widetilde x \]

from the actual solution error

\[ e=x-\widetilde x. \]

They are related by

\[ Ae=r,\qquad e=A^{-1}r. \]

Thus a small residual does not automatically guarantee a small forward error when the matrix is ill-conditioned.

These ideas reappear throghout NLA, including in the conditioning of eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

Matrix factorizations and orthogonal transformations

Distinction between QR and LU:

  • LU decomposes a matrix into triangular factors and supports the direct solution of linear systems.
  • QR constructs orthonormal bases and triangular factors, which are especially useful least squares and eigenvalue algorithms.
  • Cholesky exploits symmetric positive definiteness.

For NLA QR is more urgent than detailed LU pivoting analysis, because Ch 2 of NLA directly develops Gram-Schmidt, Householder and Givens transformations.

Basic iterative methods and convergence

Consider an iteration

\[ x^{(k+1)}=Bx^{(k)}+c. \]

If \(x^{*}\) is its fixed point, then the error satisfies

\[ e^{(k+1)}=Be^{(k)}. \]

Consequently,

\[ e^{(k)}=B^ke^{(0)}. \]

The fundamental convergence criterion is

\[ \boxed{\rho(B)<1} \]

where \(\rho(B)\) is the spectral radius.

This connects eigenvalues directly to the convergence of numerical algorithms. Jacobi and Gauss-Seidel should be revised, but with a focus on understanding how their iteration matrices arise and how their convergence can be analyzed.

The connection to the Chebyshev-filtered subspace iteration seminar

Ordinary subspace iteration

Suppose \(A\) is symmetric and we wish to compute several of its dominant eigenpairs.

We begin with a matrix \(Q_0\) whose columns are orthonormal and repeatedly compute \[ Y_{k+1}=AQ_k, \]

followed by orthonormalization, \[ Q_{k+1}=\operatorname{orth}(Y_{k+1}). \]

The successive subspaces approach an invariant subspace corresponding to dominant eigenvalues, subject to appropriate initial-subspace and spectral-gap assumptions.

Polynomial-filtered iteration

Instead of multiplying by \(A\), we apply a polynomial in \(A\): \[ Y_{k+1}=p_m(A)Q_k. \]

Why does this help?

Because for an eigenvector \(q_i\), \[ Aq_i=\lambda_iq_i \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p_m(A)q_i=p_m(\lambda_i)q_i. \]

We can therefore choose a polynomial that makes the desired eigenvalue components comparatively large and suppresses the unwanted components.

A Chebyshev polynomial, after suitable shifting and scaling, is an effective choice because of its controlled magnitude on a specified interval and rapid growth outside it.

The important point is that we can apply such a polynomial using a recurrence involving matrix–vector products, without constructing the generally dense matrix \(p_m(A)\).

The script even develops polynomial filtering in §3.3. There is an example using \[ A=\operatorname{diag}(1,2,3) \]

and the polynomial \[ p(t)=(t-1)(t-3). \]

Here the unwanted eigenvalues \(1\) and \(3\) are mapped to zero, while the component associated with eigenvalue \(2\) is retained.

Chapter 5 then combines projection, Rayleigh–Ritz approximation and Krylov-based eigenvalue methods. This is a natural starting point for comparing your seminar algorithm with Lanczos and Arnoldi methods.

One subtle but important distinction is that the seminar’s polynomial-filtered subspace method need not itself be a Krylov-subspace algorithm in the same sense as Arnoldi. Both make essential use of matrix polynomials and subspaces, but organize their iterations differently.
